Our Project

Who we are, what we're trying to do, and how OpenBat works toward it.

An initiative, not just an app

OpenBat is a community initiative for bat conservation. The app is the most visible part of it, but it exists to serve a mission rather than the other way around: build a field guide worth trusting, make bats easier for ordinary people to appreciate, and turn a night of listening into data science can actually use.

The Challenge

To quote Sir David Attenborough: “No one will protect what they don’t care about, and no one will care about what they have never experienced.” Bats are small, nocturnal, and call at frequencies humans can’t hear — for most people, they might as well be invisible.

Who we are

OpenBat is built by a few friends in Squamish, British Columbia, not a company or an institution. That shapes how the project runs: the scope stays honest about what a handful of volunteers can actually sustain, and rather than building new infrastructure from scratch, OpenBat plugs into programmes that already exist and already have the community’s trust — the BC Community Bat Programs, NABat, and iNaturalist among them.

What we’re trying to do

Three things, roughly in the order a new visitor meets them:

  1. Make appreciating bats accessible to more people — hearing a bat and learning what it might be, in the moment, with nothing more than a phone and a cheap microphone.
  2. Act as a catalyst for bat community science — turning a night of listening into a properly formatted observation that feeds iNaturalist and, through it, researchers and conservation bodies.
  3. Build a comprehensive, expert-curated field guide — the Field Guide Editor lets bat experts and enthusiasts contribute their local knowledge directly, reviewed rather than published on sight.

Bat acoustic data starts from close to empty in a lot of regions, so a simple “there are bats here, tonight” record is a meaningful step forward on its own, and it doesn’t depend on getting the species right. iNaturalist’s own research-grade standard works the same way: community agreement at whatever taxonomic depth is achievable counts, not resolution to species.

How we’re doing it

Hardware, listening apps, and community science platforms already exist as three separate pieces, and nothing connects them for someone who’s just curious. OpenBat is that connective tissue, not a competitor to any one of them, and it does three jobs at once:

  • Removes the friction between recording a bat and submitting it as usable data — record on your phone, and get a properly labelled recording, spectrogram, and metadata, ready to submit.
  • Enables a capability that doesn’t otherwise exist at this price point — live AutoID paired with the field guide lets you learn what you just heard, tied to what’s actually flying near you.
  • Recruits people passively — nobody has to “join” anything. Someone downloads a curiosity app, and the app itself introduces the idea that they could contribute to science.

None of this locks anyone into one microphone. OpenBat works with any standard USB ultrasonic mic that doesn’t need its own companion app, including Phil Atkin’s open-source Griff Mini, buildable for around $20 CAD. The rest of the stack is open too — the field guide’s species data, the detection models, and the app itself — so anyone can build on, audit, or extend any part of it without asking us. Free is a price point; open is a structural choice about who gets to build on top of this.

Not the first of its kind

The closest model isn’t another bat organisation, it’s Merlin Bird ID feeding eBird: a free app, run by Cornell Lab of Ornithology, that turns curiosity into records for a much bigger science platform, without being a conservation body itself. That’s the shape OpenBat is going for — scoped to BC first, iOS first, built by a few people rather than an institution — but aiming at the same structural gap: not a new tier in bat conservation, but the thing that makes the tiers that already exist reachable by an ordinary person.
