I'd like bats to be protected because they're astonishing animals that navigate
by shouting. That argument works on about one person in ten.

For everyone else, there's a much more boring case, and it's overwhelming.

## They eat a phenomenal quantity of insects

An insect-eating bat can consume a large fraction of its own body weight in
insects in a single night. A nursing female eats more. Multiply by a colony,
multiply by a season, and the numbers stop being intuitive.

Those insects include agricultural pests and the things that bite you in the
garden. Bats are, functionally, free overnight pest control operating across
entire landscapes, and the effect is significant enough that agricultural
researchers have gone looking for it deliberately.

The most striking framing I've come across: when bat populations crash, farmers
in affected areas measurably change their behaviour — because something has to
do the job the bats were doing, and the replacement is usually chemical.

## They pollinate things you'd miss

In warmer regions, nectar-feeding bats pollinate a long list of plants,
including a number of crops and wild plants that have evolved specifically
around them — flowers that open at night, smell strong, and are shaped for a
bat's face rather than an insect's.

Agave is the famous example, and yes, that leads where you think it leads. It's
a surprisingly effective conversation-starter.

## They plant forests

Fruit bats disperse seeds, often over long distances, and disproportionately
into cleared and degraded land that other dispersers avoid crossing.

That makes them significant agents of forest regeneration in the tropics — not a
nice extra, but part of how damaged forest comes back at all. A bat flying a
couple of kilometres and dropping seeds in a clearing is doing restoration work
nobody's paying for.

## What we lose when they go

The uncomfortable thing about all of the above is that it's mostly invisible
until it stops.

Nobody notices the insects that were eaten, the flowers that were pollinated, or
the seeds that were carried. The service is silent, distributed, and free, which
is exactly the kind of thing that gets removed without anyone deciding to remove
it.

And bats are unusually vulnerable to sudden collapse, for a reason that's worth
knowing: **they breed slowly.** Most temperate species have one pup a year.
Compare that to a small rodent producing dozens. A population knocked down by
disease, roost destruction or a bad winter takes decades to recover, if it
recovers.

That's why [[White-Nose Syndrome|a disease like white-nose syndrome]] is so
catastrophic. It's not just how many died. It's that the replacement rate is
about as slow as a mammal gets.

## The honest caveats

I don't want to oversell this, because inflated claims are how good arguments
get discredited.

The size of these effects varies enormously by region, species and crop. Studies
that put a dollar figure on bat pest control are estimates with wide error bars,
and different papers disagree by a lot. Some regions have far better evidence
than others.

What isn't in dispute: bats eat colossal numbers of insects, some plants depend
on them for pollination and dispersal, and they're a functionally significant
part of nearly every ecosystem they're in. The uncertainty is about magnitude,
not existence.

## Which brings it back to data

Here's the loop this blog keeps closing.

To argue that bats matter *here*, in this valley, on this farm, near this
development, you need to know which bats are here and how they're doing. In a
lot of places nobody knows, because nobody has looked.

That's the gap presence records fill. Not glamorous, not species-level, not
even necessarily identified — just "these are here, this is when". It's the
foundation everything else gets built on, including every argument that starts
"but this species is protected".
