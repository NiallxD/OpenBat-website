As you have seen by now, OpenBat adopts a very strong and thematic design language which might seem a little odd on initial read. In this Dev Log I want to chat a bit about why I choose this art style, and what it means for bat conservation.

## What OpenBat Feels to Me

From the very start, OpenBat felt like a grassroots initiative. It all started when I built my own low-cost bat detector, the process itself laid the foundations for the design philosophy of OpenBat.

I've always been a curious tinkerer, but I'd never done anything like this before, and I have to say that I found the process of picking and delicately placing tiny components in their places therapeutic and relaxing. While doing so I couldn't help but be amazed at engineering, technology, design, and creativity which goes into making something like this possible. 

Once the components were placed, I could drop the circuit board onto a hotplate to melt the solder and lock in the electronics. A few minutes later, my bat detector was complete. All that was left to do was upload the software to the detector and I would be able to hear bats!

This whole process to me reminded me of stories of early home and handheld gaming consoles of the early 80s. Just nerdy people tinkering with making something fun. There is undoubtedly a strong aesthetic with this era, bright colours and pixelated graphics. I feel there are some parallels with this era of gaming and what I was doing here, tinkering and building something electronic for fun! It was these factors which drew me towards the pixel art style.

## Art Style

The art style used throughout OpenBat is called "Pixel Art" or something to that effect. It features stylised text, often bold and blunt, bright and contrasty colours, and a simple and almost cartoon appearance to graphics. This art style came naturally to me while I was drafting out the first ideas of what I wanted the 'brand' to look like.

The font naturally had to speak to this language, so I opted for a blocky pixel front for the headings and titles, and a slightly less aggressive pixel-based font for the body, to aid in readability.

## Colour Palette

I have always been fond of the colour orange, the tiger is my favourite animal so that's no surprise. After spending some time browsing photographs of bats, I stumbled across the grey-headed flying fox. The orange coloured fur around it's neck and shoulders, and bright orange eyes (in the right light) locked in orange as the primary colour for OpenBat. 

For the main colours I drew inspiration from a couple of photographs I created when exploring the Indian wilderness looking for tigers. The sunsets produced this magical gradient in the sky, from orange to purple and blue.

## Art Pieces

From the start I knew I wanted mountains to be a big part of the design and brand. I'm building this from within the Coast Mountains of the Sea-to-Sky Corridor in British Columbia, it's hardly surprising mountains are on my mind. Naturally, these formed the grounding for the logo and I built the idea out from there. The mountain scape I drew found itself in the footer of the OpenBat website too.

As for the final piece, the bat, this wasn't so easy. I toyed around with a simple, bat-in-flight icon, but it never really felt like it fit. I started searching for 'Pixel art bats' and eventually found a few bats in a hanging, sleeping, position. This then got me thinking about how I could bring a sleeping (or not so) bat into the logo. After playing around with the layout, the shorter word 'bat' left room on the right for something! This naturally was the space for the bat to go, and in it went.

## Why Does This Matter for Bat Conservation?

Bats generally have a bad reputation. They are mysterious, silent, misunderstood, and in a lot of ways, vilified. Lots of bat conservation organisations take a very clean and corporate approach to their design, muted colours, silhouette logos, and blocky modern websites. I can understand why, they are presenting lots of complex information to a broad audience, and it works. But I feel that injecting some fun and character into the whole thing we start to weaken some of the arguments against bats that have long held back their conservation.

If people stop associating bats with the negative images and stories which they already hold true, we can start to chip away and turn those images and stories into something fun and exciting.
