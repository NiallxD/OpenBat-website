# Bats carry diseases. Here's what that actually means.

Most bat myths are easy to knock down. Not blind, not rodents, not interested in your hair, not vampires. All straightforwardly false, all fun to correct.

This one is different, and how you handle it matters. "Bats carry diseases" is not a myth. It's a real thing, oversimplified into something misleading, and the honest response is more persuasive than a denial.

## What's true

Bats can carry pathogens capable of infecting humans. Rabies is the one that matters most for anyone in North America or Europe who might encounter a bat directly, and it is serious. Left untreated in a human, it is nearly always fatal.

Bats also show up constantly in emerging disease research, and there are real biological reasons for that. They're numerous, long-lived for their body size, widely distributed, often live in dense colonies, and have some genuinely unusual immune characteristics that let them tolerate viral loads other mammals can't. None of that is made up. Dismissing it as hysteria just hands the argument back to whoever's skeptical.

## What's misleading about it

This is where it's worth slowing down, because the stats people actually cite are usually the wrong stats.

**The scary numbers come from a broken sample.** Rabies surveillance mostly tests bats that were submitted for testing, meaning bats that were already found grounded, sick, injured, or behaving strangely. That's not a random sample of bats, it's a sample pre-filtered toward the unhealthiest ones. Even within that skewed group, only about 5% of the nearly 24,000 bats tested by the CDC in a recent year came back positive. Compare that to raccoons (10% of those submitted), foxes (19%), and skunks (20%). Bats test positive at a lower rate than several of the classic rabies carriers. They just get submitted for testing far more often, because people notice a grounded bat and walk past a healthy raccoon without a second thought.

**In the actual wild population, prevalence is well under 1%.** Studies that survey healthy, randomly sampled bats in their habitat, rather than the bats people bring in for testing, consistently find infection rates below 1%. The 5 to 10% figures that circulate are true only for the small, sick, non-representative slice of bats that end up in a lab.

> **An Analogy**
> Imagine walking into a doctors clinic and testing everyone in the foyer for some kind of illness or disease. Then report that stat as 'the percentage of humans with an illness is...'. That what we see with bats. Only the ill or misbehaving ones are tested, skewing the data.

**The risk to you is about contact, not proximity.** Living near bats, watching them, listening to them on a detector, none of that transmits anything. Handling a bat, or being bitten or scratched by one, is the route that matters, and it's avoidable simply by not handling bats, which you shouldn't be doing anyway.

**"Bats" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.** There are around 1,400 species across most of the planet. Generalising about their disease ecology is about as useful as generalising about "birds."

> **Bird Illness**
> Birds are behind a fair amount of everyday human illness, mostly through West Nile virus (about 2,000 US cases and 120 deaths a year, via mosquitoes that pick it up from birds), plus poultry-linked Salmonella and occasional psittacosis. Bird flu gets the headlines, but it's actually rare, under 1,000 confirmed human cases worldwide since 2003. So birds trend toward a steady, low-severity drip of illness rather than bats' rare, high-fatality spikes.

**The death toll, where rabies control exists, is tiny.** In the US, bats are the leading source of the handful of human rabies deaths still recorded there, typically one to three a year. Set that against the global rabies burden of roughly 59,000 deaths annually, which is overwhelmingly driven by unvaccinated domestic dogs in Asia and Africa. Bats carry a lot of the fear and very little of the actual global mortality.

**The human side is usually the deciding factor in spillover.** Where a pathogen actually jumps from bats to people, it tends to involve situations that force intense, sustained, close contact: habitat destruction pushing animals into farmland, wildlife trade and markets, disturbance of roosts. The animal isn't the variable. The contact is.

**Culling makes it worse, not better.** This is the practical part that gets lost. Destroying roosts and killing colonies disperses animals, stresses them, and increases movement between groups, which can spread pathogens further rather than containing them. It also removes the pest control and pollination those colonies were providing for free. It's the intervention most likely to feel decisive and most likely to backfire.

## The rule for anyone with a detector

Simple, and it covers essentially all of it.

**Do not touch bats.**

If you find a grounded, injured, or apparently sick bat, contact a local bat group, wildlife rescue, or your public health service. They'll know what's appropriate where you live, and people who handle bats professionally are vaccinated and trained for exactly this.

If you are bitten or scratched by a bat, seek medical advice promptly. Not "keep an eye on it." Promptly, even if you're not certain. Don't take the risk. This is one of the few pieces of advice here where the stakes justify the bluntness.

That's the entire risk management protocol for a bat detecting hobby. Stand outside, listen, don't touch.

## Why the perception problem is a conservation problem

Public feeling about bats has real consequences. Roosts get destroyed. Colonies get evicted or killed. Local support for protection evaporates. This is measurable, after periods of intense negative coverage, bat conservation organisations report exactly these effects.

That's why the conservation literature keeps naming public perception as one of the two big structural problems facing bats, alongside the lack of good population data. It's not a soft, PR-adjacent concern. It changes what actually happens to real colonies.

## How do we address the perception problem?

Hand somebody a detector on a summer evening and let them hear a bat go past. The animal stops being an abstract disease vector and becomes a specific small creature doing a difficult job overhead, and the conversation afterwards is completely different from the one you'd have had by citing studies.

That's the whole reason this project spends as much time on the "make it easy to be curious" half as on the data half. Facts are necessary. They're also, reliably, not sufficient.

> [!note] The honest summary
> Bats can carry rabies, and it's genuinely dangerous if you're bitten or scratched. The prevalence in wild bat populations is well under 1%, and the scary numbers you've heard almost certainly come from a biased sample of already-sick bats. The risk to anyone who doesn't handle bats is very low. Don't handle them. If you're bitten, get medical care immediately, no exceptions. Killing or evicting bats makes the underlying problem worse and removes an animal doing significant work for free. All of that is true at once, and saying all of it is more convincing than saying only the comfortable half.

## Last off, a little bit of *fun*

**Top 10 things more likely to kill an average American family member per year than a rabid bat (1 to 3 deaths a year), just from daily life:**

1. **Wildlife-vehicle collisions (mostly deer)**: 440+ deaths/year, just from the regular commute
2. **Portable generators (carbon monoxide)**: 70 to 100 deaths/year, from running one during a storm power outage
3. **Lawn mowers**: ~90 deaths/year, ordinary Saturday yard work
4. **Bee and wasp stings**: ~72 deaths/year, mostly allergic reactions nobody knew they had
5. **Dog attacks**: ~43 deaths/year, often the family dog or a neighbour's
6. **Furniture or TV tip-overs**: ~29 deaths/year, almost entirely toddlers pulling a dresser onto themselves
7. **Lightning strikes**: ~28 deaths/year, being outside when a summer storm rolls in
8. **Fireworks**: ~10 deaths/year, the Fourth of July backyard tradition
9. **Escalators and elevators**: ~5 to 6 deaths/year, a routine trip to the mall or airport
10. **Venomous snakebites**: ~5 deaths/year, gardening or mowing near the wrong patch of grass
