Bat detecting goes quiet in winter, and new detector owners often assume their
gear has broken. It hasn't. The bats have gone into the most precarious few
months of their year, and what they're doing is genuinely strange.

## Hibernation is not sleeping

A hibernating bat isn't napping. It has deliberately shut itself down.

Body temperature drops to close to the temperature of the surroundings. Heart
rate falls from hundreds of beats a minute to a handful. Breathing can pause for
long stretches. Metabolism drops to a tiny fraction of normal.

The point of all this is arithmetic. In autumn, an insect-eating bat in a
temperate region faces a simple problem: for several months there will be
essentially nothing to eat. Flying costs an enormous amount of energy. Staying
warm costs a lot too. So the strategy is to eat hard in autumn, find somewhere
cold, stable and humid, and then spend the winter burning as little as possible.

The fat reserve it goes in with has to last until spring. That's the whole game.

## Why disturbance is so costly

Here's the consequence that matters for anyone poking around in winter.

A hibernating bat that's disturbed has to warm itself back up, and warming up
from near-ambient to flight temperature is expensive — a single arousal can burn
a meaningful chunk of the fat that was meant to last months. In January there is
nothing to eat, so that fat cannot be replaced.

Bats do rouse naturally, occasionally, and on mild nights some species will fly
and even feed. But every *extra* arousal is a withdrawal from an account that
can't be topped up.

This is why entering caves, mines and roosts in winter is restricted in many
places, and why "I only had a quick look" is not a harmless sentence. You can do
real damage without touching anything, just by being warm and present and
noisy.

## Which is exactly what makes white-nose syndrome so lethal

The mechanism behind [[White-Nose Syndrome|the disease]] that has killed North
American bats in staggering numbers is precisely this arithmetic being broken.

The fungus grows on hibernating bats in cold, humid conditions. It irritates
them, and irritated bats rouse more often. More arousals means more energy burnt,
which means the reserve runs out weeks before spring — and a bat that wakes
starving in February goes looking for insects that don't exist yet.

The animals aren't killed by the fungus directly so much as by the budget it
wrecks. Understanding hibernation is what makes that awful clarity click.

## Not everyone hibernates

Some species migrate instead, and a few do both to varying degrees.

Migration swaps one risk for another: instead of the fat-budget problem, you get
the journey — exposure, exhaustion, unfamiliar territory, and in the modern
landscape, wind turbines, which are a significant cause of death for some
migratory species precisely because they're operating in open air at the same
altitudes.

Neither strategy is safe. They're different bets against the same problem of
winter.

## What this means for your recording year

**Don't judge your equipment in January.** A blank winter evening tells you
nothing about your microphone.

**Mild winter nights are worth trying**, occasionally. Some species will fly, and
winter activity records are unusually scarce — which makes them unusually
valuable.

**Autumn is interesting.** Feeding activity as animals fatten up, and in some
species mating behaviour, produce a distinctive and busy period that's less
watched than midsummer.

**Spring emergence is the one to catch.** The first bats of the year, after
months of nothing, are hungry and active. If you're going to record consistently
at one site, the transition from nothing to something is the most informative
part of the whole year — and the date it happens is exactly the kind of thing
long-term records can answer and one-off visits cannot.

> [!warning] The winter rule
> Whatever you do between roughly November and March, don't go looking inside
> anywhere bats might be hibernating. Not caves, not mines, not lofts, not
> tunnels. If you want to be involved in winter surveys, contact your local bat
> group — those counts exist, they're done carefully by people trained and
> licensed for it, and they'd probably love another pair of hands doing it
> properly.
