Nothing I have done for bats has been as effective as standing in a park and
saying "here, listen to this" to a stranger.

A bat walk is that, organised. It's the highest-leverage two hours available to
anyone who owns a detector, and it doesn't require you to be an expert — it
requires you to be about one evening ahead of everyone else there.

## Before the night

**Recce the route in daylight, then again at dusk.** You need to know where the
bats reliably are, and you need to know where the tree roots are. Do both.

**Keep it short and flat.** An hour of walking is plenty. People will be looking
up, in the dark, often with children, sometimes with a phone in one hand. Choose
somewhere you'd be happy to walk backwards along.

**Pick water if you can.** A pond or slow river with trees around it will
out-perform anything else, and reliability matters far more on a bat walk than
variety. A blank evening in an interesting place is worse than a busy evening in
a boring one.

**Start before sunset.** Meet in daylight, do the talking bit while people can
still see each other's faces, and be in position as the light goes. If you start
in the dark you'll spend the first twenty minutes doing admin by torchlight.

**Have a wet-weather answer.** Either an indoor alternative or a clear "we'll
cancel by 4pm if it's blowing", decided in advance.

## What to bring

- **More detectors than you think.** One between two is transformative. One for
  fifteen people is a queue.
- **Spare battery packs.** Phones running audio processing with the screen on
  will not survive two hours on 40%.
- **A red torch**, and a spare, and the discipline to keep white light off.
- **Something to sit on** if you're doing a static emergence count.
- **A rough species list** for the area, so you can say "that'll probably be a
  such-and-such" without inventing anything.

## The ten-minute talk at the start

Keep it short, and front-load the two facts that change how people listen:

1. **You cannot hear bats.** Explain that the detector is a translator, not an
   amplifier. This lands better than any other single sentence — the idea that
   there's a whole layer of sound above human hearing is genuinely startling to
   most people.
2. **Bats are not what you were told.** Not blind, not rodents, not going to get
   in your hair, and there are around 1,400 species doing wildly different jobs.
   Thirty seconds, not a lecture.

Then say what's about to happen: it'll be quiet, then suddenly it won't, and
when it isn't, look up.

## During

**Let the detector do the talking.** Resist narrating over it. The first pass of
the night should be heard, not explained.

**Name the buzz.** When you get a feeding buzz, say what it is straight away —
a bat closing in on an insect. It's the moment people remember, and it's the one
that turns "neat gadget" into "I just heard something hunt".

**Be honest about identification.** "That's probably X, but several species
sound similar and I wouldn't swear to it" is a much better answer than a
confident wrong name, and it teaches people something true about the field. If
you're using OpenBat, the confidence and the "sounds alike" flags do this work
for you — show people the screen.

**Watch the group, not the sky.** Someone will be cold, someone will be lost at
the back, and someone will have a question they're too shy to ask. That last one
is usually the person who becomes the volunteer.

## Finishing well

The last five minutes decide whether anything happens afterwards. Do three
things:

1. **Say what you heard**, plainly, and how many passes.
2. **Point at what's next.** Your local bat group, community bat programme, or
   the survey that needs volunteers. Have the name ready — this is the handoff
   that turns an evening into a participant.
3. **Tell them what they can do at home.** Turn the outside light off. Leave a
   corner of the garden messy. Report a roost rather than disturbing it. Small,
   concrete, achievable.

> [!tip] The line that works
> "Everything you just heard was happening every night, over your street,
> whether or not anyone was listening." People go quiet for a second after that
> one.

## If you'd rather not organise anything

Then don't. Take a detector to a barbecue. Hand it to a neighbour over the
fence. Bring it on a camping trip and pass it round after dark.

Every person who hears a bat for the first time is a small, permanent change to
how they think about the animal, and there is no minimum group size for that.
