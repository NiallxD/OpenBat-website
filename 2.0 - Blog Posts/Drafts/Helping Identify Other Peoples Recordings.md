Everything else on this blog is about producing records. This one's about the
other side of the transaction, which gets far less attention and is arguably
where the shortage actually is.

Somebody has to look at all those uploads. On iNaturalist, that somebody is
whoever turns up.

## Why it's short-handed

Bat acoustics has a much smaller identifier community than birds or plants. The
skill is less common, the evidence is harder to read, and a fair proportion of
uploads can't be resolved past genus anyway, which makes it less immediately
rewarding than confirming a photo of a robin.

The result is a backlog: recordings sitting unreviewed, sometimes for a long
time. Every one of those is a person who took the trouble to contribute and got
silence back — which is, incidentally, the single most effective way to stop
somebody contributing again.

## You can help before you can identify

This is the part people don't realise. Useful contributions on an unreviewed
observation, roughly in order of how much expertise they need:

**Confirm it's a bat at all.** Plenty of uploads are crickets, machinery or
rain. Saying so, politely, with a reason, is genuinely useful and requires
only what you'd learn in
[[What Your Detector Hears That Is Not A Bat|a couple of evenings]].

**Agree at a coarse level.** "This is a bat" — order-level — is a real
identification and moves the record forward.

**Ask for the audio.** Lots of bat observations go up as a spectrogram image
only. A polite "would you be able to attach the WAV as well?" makes the
observation reviewable by someone who can go further.

**Point out what's missing.** A truncated call, a spectrogram with no frequency
axis, a missing time. All fixable by the uploader if somebody mentions it.

**Explain why it's hard.** If a call falls in a genuinely ambiguous group,
saying so — and naming the group — is more valuable than leaving it blank. It
teaches the uploader something and stops the record being wrongly resolved later.

## Getting better at it

If you want to move from "that's a bat" to something more specific:

**Start with your own recordings**, where you know the site, the season and the
habitat. Context does a lot of work that acoustics alone can't.

**Learn your local species list first.** Five or six species you might actually
encounter is a tractable problem. Fourteen hundred is not.

**Get reference recordings.** Your local bat group will have them, and there are
published libraries. Comparing an unknown against verified examples from *your
region* beats any reference table.

**Look at sequences, not calls.**
[[What The Numbers Mean|A single call is weak evidence]]. The spread across a
pass is where the information is.

**Be around people who know more.** This is the accelerator. A bat group evening
where someone talks you through what they're seeing is worth months of solo
squinting.

## The etiquette

Identifying other people's records is a social activity, and it goes wrong in
predictable ways.

**Say why, not just what.** "*Myotis* sp. — the sweep is too steep for a
Lasiurus and the tail's cut off, so I wouldn't go further" teaches something.
A bare species name teaches nothing and can't be argued with.

**Be comfortable being unsure.** "I think this is X but wouldn't bet on it" is a
perfectly respectable comment. Overconfident identification is how bad records
get into permanent databases.

**Don't ignore beginners.** The first observation someone uploads is the one
where a friendly comment decides whether there's a second one.

**Disagree gently and specifically.** You're often correcting someone who was
excited. It costs nothing to be nice about it.

## Why this closes the loop

The project's whole argument is that bat records are scarce and therefore
valuable. But an unreviewed record is only half a record — it sits there, not
research grade, not flowing onward to the wider database, not counting for
anything.

The identifiers are what turn recordings into data. They're volunteers,
there aren't enough of them, and the entry requirements are lower than everyone
assumes.

If you've been doing this for a season and can reliably tell a bat from a
cricket, you're already useful. Go and be useful.

> [!tip] Fifteen minutes
> Next time you upload something, spend fifteen minutes looking at other
> people's unreviewed bat observations in your region before you close the tab.
> Do that each time and you'll have paid your own way — and you'll get much
> better at reading calls into the bargain.
