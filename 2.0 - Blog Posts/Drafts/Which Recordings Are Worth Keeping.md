Arm the recorder on a busy night and you'll come home with a lot of files. The
temptation is to treat all of them as data and upload the lot.

Don't. A hundred mediocre recordings is not ten times more useful than ten good
ones — it's less useful, because somebody has to wade through them, and
identifiers are a scarcer resource than recordings.

Here's how I triage a night.

## Start with triggered display

Open the session and switch the spectrogram to **triggered display mode**, which
throws away the silence and shows the pulses back to back. Four hours of a quiet
night becomes something you can scan in a minute.

You're not identifying anything yet. You're just looking for the passes that
have shape — clean sweeps rather than smears, several calls in sequence rather
than one lonely fragment.

## What makes a recording worth uploading

In rough order of importance:

**Several calls in a row.** A single call is weak evidence. A sequence lets an
identifier see how the animal was calling and whether the shape is consistent —
and consistency across a pass is worth far more than one impressive-looking
pulse.

**Clean, complete sweeps.** The whole call visible: the top of the sweep, the
body, the tail. If the top's missing, the bat was probably distant and the high
frequencies faded before they reached you.

**Quiet background.** No wind roar, no cricket carpet, no rustling jacket.

**Something interesting happening.** A feeding buzz, a social call, an unusual
shape. These are the recordings people actually enjoy looking at.

**Context you can supply.** Where, when, what the weather was, what habitat.
Cheap to add, disproportionately useful.

## What to bin, or at least not upload

- **Single-pulse fragments.** Fine as "a bat was here", not worth an
  identifier's time.
- **Anything you can't tell from a cricket.** Repetitive, scratchy, and going on
  for ages? Not a bat.
- **Wind and rain recordings.** The detector triggered on noise.
- **The forty near-identical passes from the same bat working the same pond.**
  Keep the best two or three. The others are the same information again.

> [!tip] Keep more than you upload
> Binning and not-uploading are different decisions. Storage is cheap and your
> own archive is how you learn — the recording that baffles you tonight will make
> sense next summer. Upload the best; keep the rest on your phone or a drive.

## The two-question test

Before uploading anything, ask:

1. **Could someone else check this?** If the evidence isn't in the file, you're
   asking people to take your word for it, and that's not what the platform is
   for.
2. **Does it add something the others don't?** New site, new date, new species,
   new behaviour, better quality. If it's the fifth near-identical pass, it
   doesn't.

## Quality and confidence are different questions

Worth repeating because it's the most common muddle:
[[Two Scores That Look The Same|pulse quality and species confidence measure different things]].

A crisp recording of a bat nobody can identify is a **great** upload — high
quality, low confidence, exactly what an identifier can work with. A scrappy
recording with a confident species name is the one to be suspicious of.

So don't filter by whether the app gave you a name. Filter by whether the
recording is good.

## And the ones that are just for you

Not every recording needs a purpose. Some of them you keep because it was the
first bat of the year, or because you were somewhere lovely, or because the buzz
was so fast it made you laugh out loud.

The archive of a season is worth having for its own sake. The subset you upload
is a different, smaller thing with a different job — and being clear about which
is which makes both of them better.
