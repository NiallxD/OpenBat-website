If you take one practical thing from this whole blog, make it this one: **go and
find the bat people near you.**

They exist nearly everywhere, they've usually been doing this for years, and
they are almost always short of hands. An app can tell you what you heard. It
cannot tell you that the colony under the bridge moved last spring, or lend you
a detector, or take you to the roost count.

## What's actually out there

The structure varies a lot by country, which is why "find your local bat group"
is better advice than any specific link.

**In British Columbia**, where OpenBat lives, there's a provincial community bat
programme running through regional centres, with trained volunteer bat
ambassadors and thousands of public reports logged. It's coordinated, staffed by
biologists, and plugged into government monitoring.

**Across Canada**, it's a federation — other provinces run their own equivalent
programmes rather than there being one national bat body.

**In the UK**, it's shaped differently: a national charity with a large network
of local bat groups underneath it, most of which run walks, training and
surveys.

**In the US**, monitoring largely runs through the North American Bat Monitoring
Program, with state and regional groups alongside it.

Elsewhere, search for your country plus "bat group", "bat conservation" or "bat
monitoring". University biology departments and natural history societies are
also good doors in.

## What you get out of it

**Local knowledge you cannot get any other way.** Which species are actually
around, which sites are worth your time, what the calls look like *here* — which
is not always what the reference material says.

**Equipment.** Many groups have detectors to lend. If you're hesitating over
buying one, this solves it.

**Training.** Roost counts, transect surveys, and eventually — if you want it —
the licensing route for work that involves handling or entering roosts.

**Somewhere for your data to matter.** A record on iNaturalist is useful. A
record that also reaches the people running your regional monitoring is more
useful. They often want the same observations, and they'll tell you exactly
what's helpful.

**People to be baffled with.** This hobby involves standing in the dark being
puzzled by squiggles. It is much better with company.

## What they get out of you

I want to be straight about this, because "volunteer with us" can sound like
charity in one direction.

Most bat groups are small, ageing, and stretched. They have more sites than
surveyors, more reports than people to follow them up, and a persistent problem
recruiting anyone under about fifty. Someone turning up who is genuinely
interested, reasonably technical, and willing to stand in a field is not a
burden — it's the thing they've been trying to arrange for years.

You don't need expertise. You need to show up twice.

## How OpenBat fits alongside them

Deliberately, as a tool rather than a rival. We're not trying to become a bat
group, run a monitoring programme, or hold anyone's data. Those organisations
have the expertise, the relationships and the trust, and they should keep them.

What we're trying to be is the thing that makes contributing easier for someone
who isn't already a trained volunteer — the on-ramp, not the road. If you use
OpenBat and then end up doing proper surveys with a local group, the app has
worked exactly as intended, and you should absolutely stop reading our
documentation and start listening to them.

> [!tip] The email to send
> "Hi — I've got a bat detector and I'm interested in local bats. Is there
> anything coming up I could come along to?" That's it. That's the whole
> message. I have never known it go unanswered.

## And if there's genuinely nothing near you?

Some places really don't have anything organised, and that's often exactly where
records are most scarce.

In that case: keep recording, upload to iNaturalist so the data is somewhere
durable, and consider being the person who starts something. A bat walk for six
friends is a beginning, and most of the groups above started as roughly that.
