Open the field guide and you get a globe. Spin it and there are regions
everywhere, some of them full, some of them showing a big fat zero.

That zero is honest, and it's deliberate, and I want to explain the thinking
because "global app, regional content" looks like a contradiction until you see
the arithmetic.

## The tempting version

The pitch that writes itself is: *a comprehensive, expert-vetted field guide to
every bat species on Earth, in your pocket, free.* Nobody has built it. It would
be genuinely valuable. It makes a lovely slide.

Now cost it out. Around 1,400 species. Each needs a written profile, correct
measurements, call characteristics, distribution, conservation status, a usable
photograph with clear licensing, and someone qualified to check all of it.
Taxonomy shifts constantly, so it needs maintaining forever, not writing once.

That is not a project. That's an institution, with staff, and funding, and a
succession plan.

We are a few friends in Squamish with day jobs.

## What we can actually be responsible for

So we inverted the question. Instead of "what would be impressive?", we asked
"what can we stand behind indefinitely?"

The answer was British Columbia. All the province's bat species are in the
guide. There's an existing network of people here who know these animals —
community bat programmes, trained volunteers, biologists — which means there are
real reviewers within reach, not hypothetical ones. And it's where we live,
which matters more than it sounds: we can go and check things.

## Why the app is still available everywhere

Because there's no reason to punish someone in Portugal for our review capacity.

The detector works anywhere. The spectrogram works anywhere. The recording,
the measurements, the sessions, the export to community science — all of that is
physics and file formats, and physics doesn't care which province you're in.

What varies by region is **identification** (only where an open model exists —
currently North America, with the UK in beta) and **guide content** (only where
someone has written and checked it).

So someone downloading in a region we don't cover gets a real bat detector with
an incomplete guide, rather than a locked screen. That's a fair deal and it's
stated plainly rather than hidden.

## The zeros are an invitation

Here's my favourite consequence of doing it this way. Those zeros on the globe
aren't a gap we're embarrassed by; they're the most effective recruitment device
in the app.

If you live somewhere showing zero species and you know your local bats, you are
looking at a very direct request for help. That's a much better feeling than
finding a thin, wrong, auto-generated entry for your area and having to argue
with it.

We could have filled every region with plausible-sounding text in an afternoon.
It would have looked complete and been worthless, and it would have destroyed
the credibility of the parts that *are* right.

## What "going wider" looks like

Not a big bang. Province by province, region by region, roughly in this order:

1. Somewhere there's a bat person who wants their region covered.
2. They contribute entries through the guide editor.
3. Someone qualified reviews them.
4. That region stops being a zero.

Which means the honest answer to "when will you cover my area?" is: **when
somebody there decides to make it happen, and we'll help.** That's not a dodge.
It's how a guide built on real expertise has to grow, and it's why the
[Contribute](/contribute/) page exists.

> [!note] The comparison we keep coming back to
> Merlin Bird ID didn't launch covering every bird on Earth. It started with a
> region, did it properly, and expanded as the data and the reviewers arrived.
> It's now one of the most successful nature apps ever made. Slow and correct
> beats fast and hollow.
