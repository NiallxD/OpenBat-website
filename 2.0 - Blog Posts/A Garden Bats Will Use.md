Most "wildlife gardening" advice involves buying things and doing work. This one
mostly involves doing less, which is why I like it.

Bats want three things from a garden. Get those three roughly right and you can
skip everything else.

## 1. Insects

A bat is not interested in your garden. It's interested in what's flying in it.

The single biggest lever is **stopping doing things that reduce insects**:

- **Don't spray.** Insecticides work, which is the problem.
- **Let some of it go.** A patch of long grass, a corner left unmown, a few
  nettles behind the shed. Insect numbers respond quickly to this.
- **Leave the dead stuff.** A log pile, a heap of prunings, leaves under a hedge.
  Rotting material feeds a whole chain that ends in something flying.

Then, if you want to actively help:

- **Night-scented flowers.** Honeysuckle, evening primrose, night-scented stock —
  anything that smells strongest after dark is advertising to moths.
- **A pond.** Even a washing-up bowl sunk in the ground produces insects. A
  proper small pond is the single best thing you can add to a garden for
  wildlife generally, and bats benefit at second hand.

## 2. Dark

Bats won't use a floodlit garden, and
[[Turn The Light Off|some species won't cross a lit gap at all]].

- Turn outside lights off, or put them on a motion sensor.
- Point what's left downwards, with a hood.
- Keep the boundary dark, especially if a hedge or tree line runs along it.

This one's free, immediate, and you get stars back.

## 3. A route in

Bats commute along features. A garden that connects to a hedge, a tree line, a
stream or a row of mature trees is reachable. One surrounded by fence panels and
lit paving is, from a bat's point of view, in the middle of the sea.

- **Keep the hedge**, especially if it links to anything else green.
- **Height helps.** Trees and tall shrubs give shelter and a flight line.
- **Continuity matters more than size.** A thin, unbroken, unlit corridor beats a
  big isolated patch.

## What about bat boxes?

Reasonable question, and the honest answer is: they can work, they often don't,
and they're the least important thing on this page.

If you want to try one:

- **Height and aspect matter.** Generally high up, unobstructed, with a clear
  flight path in, and somewhere that gets warmth without cooking.
- **Boxes take time.** Occupation can take years, and many boxes are never
  occupied at all.
- **Design and placement are regional.** What works depends on your species and
  climate, which is exactly why I'm not going to give you dimensions from the
  other side of the world.

Ask your local bat group. They will know which designs are used locally, where
they've worked, and whether anybody's monitoring boxes in your area — and
they'll be pleased you asked rather than nailing up something from the internet.

**And once a box is occupied, it's a roost**, with everything that implies:
protected in many countries, not to be opened, disturbed, or peered into.
Watching from a distance at dusk is the way to check it.

## The best bit: you can measure it

This is where owning a detector changes the exercise from faith to evidence.

Record in your garden regularly — the same spot, roughly the same time relative
to sunset, whenever conditions are reasonable. That's your baseline.

Then change something. Switch off the security light. Let the back third grow
out. Dig the pond. Keep recording.

You won't get a clean scientific result — too many variables, too few nights —
but over a couple of seasons you'll get something far more motivating than a
leaflet: your own before-and-after, from your own garden.

> [!tip] Start with the light
> If you do exactly one thing from this post, make it the outside light. It's
> free, it takes ten seconds, it's reversible, and it's the change most likely to
> show up on your detector within a week.

## And if you don't have a garden

Balconies, window boxes and a dark window are all worth something, and a
communal green space near you is worth more.

Honestly though, the highest-impact version for a flat-dweller isn't habitat
at all — it's
[[How To Run A Bat Walk|taking a detector out and showing someone else]]. One
converted neighbour with a garden beats a very good window box.
