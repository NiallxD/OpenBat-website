You can have the best microphone in the county and a perfectly chosen spot, and
still hear nothing, because you went out on the wrong night.

Timing is the biggest lever in bat detecting and it costs nothing. Here's how to
use it.

## Within a night: the two windows

Most insect-eating bats leave the roost around sunset and are busiest in the
couple of hours that follow. Activity then usually drops through the middle of
the night, and picks up again before dawn as bats return.

OpenBat shows you where you are in that cycle in the top-left of the detector
screen — sunset or sunrise, how long until it, or how long since. The sun icon
is filled while you're inside an active window and hollow outside one.

The clever bit, which I like more than I should: **the windows aren't a fixed
number of hours.** They're defined as the first and last fifteen percent of the
night. That means they stretch and shrink with the season — close to an hour and
three quarters either side around the equinoxes, nearer an hour at midsummer
when the whole night is only seven hours long.

A fixed "two hours after sunset" would be wrong for most of the year in most
places. A percentage of the actual night tracks reality.

## Across a year: the season

This is where a lot of people come unstuck, particularly if they buy a detector
in autumn and conclude it doesn't work.

In temperate regions, bats are strongly seasonal. Broadly:

- **Winter** — hibernation. Bats may rouse briefly on mild nights, but expect
  very little. A blank January evening tells you nothing about your equipment.
- **Spring** — emergence, and hungry bats after months of not eating. Activity
  builds quickly.
- **Mid to late summer** — the peak. Young bats are flying for the first time,
  which roughly doubles the population in the air and adds a lot of erratic,
  inexperienced flying to watch.
- **Autumn** — feeding up, mating in some species, then a fairly sharp
  fall-off.

If you're starting out, start in summer. You'll learn far more in three good
July evenings than in a whole grim winter of nothing.

## The weather, in order of how much it matters

**Temperature.** Insects need warmth. Cold nights mean fewer insects, which means
less reason for a bat to spend energy flying. Warm, still evenings are the ones
to clear your diary for.

**Wind.** Bad twice over: bats avoid exposed flying in wind, and wind across a
microphone produces a roar of low-frequency noise that buries everything else.
If it's blowing, get behind something — a hedge, a wall, a building — and let it
shelter both you and the mic.

**Rain.** Steady rain generally means very quiet. Light drizzle is survivable.
Rain on the microphone is not, and neither is rain in your phone's charging
port, so pack accordingly.

**Cloud.** Slight positive if anything — cloudy nights hold warmth. Don't wait
for a clear sky.

## Consistency beats conditions

Here's the thing that's easy to miss: if you only go out on perfect nights,
your records tell you about perfect nights.

The most valuable thing you can do — for your own understanding as much as for
anyone else's data — is go to the same place regularly, in whatever weather, and
record what you find, *including the blanks*. A run of quiet nights either side
of a busy one is what turns a scattering of observations into something you can
actually reason about.

> [!tip] Log the nothing
> A session with no detections is a real result. It's the only way anyone can
> later tell the difference between "no bats" and "nobody looked".

## A quick planning routine

1. Check sunset time and aim to be set up half an hour before it.
2. Check the forecast for wind first, temperature second.
3. Pick somewhere sheltered if it's breezy.
4. Give it at least an hour past sunset before drawing any conclusions.
5. If it's dead, note the temperature and wind before you leave — future-you
   will want them.

Do that half a dozen times in a summer and you'll have a much better sense of
your local bats than any amount of reading, including this.
