Turn on a bat detector for the first time and the striking thing isn't how many
bats there are. It's how loud the world is up there.

The ultrasonic range is not the silent void you'd assume. It's full of insects,
machinery, friction and weather, none of which you've ever heard because your
ears politely stopped reporting decades ago. Learning to dismiss all of it is
half the skill.

## The usual suspects

**Bush crickets and grasshoppers.** The number one false alarm, comfortably.
Many are genuinely ultrasonic and genuinely loud. On the spectrogram they show
up as dense, scratchy, repetitive blocks rather than clean sweeps, and the giveaway
is persistence — a cricket will happily keep going for twenty minutes. Bats do
not.

**Keys, zips and buckles.** Any sharp metallic contact produces a click that
contains every frequency at once, which draws a vertical smear right up the
spectrogram. If your "bat" is a straight vertical line, check your pockets.

**Fabric.** Waterproof jackets are the worst offenders. Every arm movement is a
broadband rustle. This is why standing still matters more than you'd think, and
why your first ten minutes with a new coat are full of imaginary bats.

**Rain and wind.** Rain on or near the microphone is a continuous crackle of
impacts. Wind is a low roar that lifts the whole bottom of the picture and
buries anything quiet. Neither is fixable in software; get behind a hedge.

**Electronics.** LED drivers, phone chargers, cheap dimmer switches, some
motion sensors and a fair amount of shop lighting whine in the ultrasonic range.
The tell is inhuman consistency — a perfectly flat, perfectly steady line at one
frequency. Nothing alive holds a note that well.

**Moths.** Some moths click, and some of those clicks are anti-bat defences —
they exist specifically to interfere with echolocation, which is a wonderful
thing to detect by accident.

**Water.** Fast-flowing water is broadband noise. A babbling brook is charming
and acoustically hopeless.

## How to tell the difference at a glance

| What you see | What it probably is |
|---|---|
| A short, curved sweep from high to low | A bat |
| A vertical line covering every frequency | Something mechanical — a click |
| A dense scratchy block, repeating steadily for ages | An insect |
| A perfectly flat, unchanging line | Electronics |
| A rising wash across the bottom of the screen | Wind |
| Speckled noise everywhere | Rain, or the gain set very high |

The single most useful test: **bats are brief and they leave.** If it's been
there for more than a couple of seconds without changing, it isn't a bat.

## Tuning the detector to ignore the rubbish

Most false triggers can be dealt with in **Settings > Detecting**.

**Use "Loudness + pitch" rather than "Loudness".** Loudness-only counts anything
loud enough, whatever its pitch, which means every door and every footstep. Add
the pitch requirement and most of the mechanical world disappears.

**Set the lowest pitch sensibly.** Anything below the threshold is ignored
however loud it is. 15 to 20 kHz clears wind and handling noise while keeping
essentially every bat. Push it up to 30 or 40 kHz and you'll target only the
higher-pitched species — useful occasionally, but you're now deliberately
throwing away the low callers.

**Adjust loudness to your site.** The threshold matches the brightness scale on
the spectrogram, so anything brighter than your setting triggers. Lower it to
pick up faint, distant bats, and accept more noise with them. Raise it on a
windy night.

**Shortest call** rejects clicks and pops by requiring a sound to last a
sensible length of time. **Join gaps up to** handles the opposite problem: a
real call can dip quiet in the middle, and without bridging that gap one call
gets logged as three.

> [!tip] Tune once, in the field, on a bad night
> The best time to set your thresholds is a windy evening with crickets going.
> If the detector is quiet then, it'll be clean on a good night. Tuning in a
> silent room teaches you nothing.

## Don't over-tune

There's a temptation to keep tightening until nothing but perfect calls get
through. Resist it.

Every filter that rejects noise also rejects some genuine bats — the distant
ones, the quiet ones, the species calling at frequencies near your threshold. A
completely clean detector is usually a detector missing things.

Better to accept some rubbish and review it afterwards. Triggered display mode
makes that quick: it skips the silence and shows only the pulses back to back,
so scanning a long session for the real ones takes a minute rather than an hour.

Learning what isn't a bat is, oddly, one of the more satisfying parts of this.
It means you're not squinting at a screen hoping — you're reading it.
