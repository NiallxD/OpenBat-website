The literature on bat conservation names two big structural problems over and
over: there isn't enough data, and people don't like bats.

The first one is a tooling problem and most of this blog is about it. The second
one is a story problem, and it's arguably harder, because you can't fix a
feeling with a spreadsheet.

So here are the myths I hear most, and what's actually true.

## "Bats are blind"

They are not. Every bat species has eyes and can see, and some see very well
indeed. The large fruit bats navigate mainly by sight and have excellent vision.

What's remarkable isn't that bats can't see — it's that the insect-hunting
species evolved a *second* way of perceiving the world that works in total
darkness, at speed, in three dimensions, accurate enough to catch a moth in
mid-air. They didn't replace vision. They added to it.

## "Bats are flying mice"

Not remotely. Bats aren't rodents and aren't closely related to them. They're
their own order, Chiroptera, which is one of the largest groups of mammals on
Earth — roughly one in five mammal species is a bat.

The resemblance is superficial and mostly about size and fur. Genetically,
you're looking at an animal about as related to a mouse as a cat is.

## "Bats will get in your hair"

An animal that can detect and intercept an insect the size of a grain of rice,
in the dark, at 30 km/h, is not going to blunder into your head.

When a bat swoops near you, it's almost always chasing insects — which are
attracted to you, because you're warm and you breathe out carbon dioxide.
You're not the target. You're the bait.

## "They're all vampires"

Of the roughly 1,400 bat species, three feed on blood, all in Latin America,
and they feed mostly on livestock and birds.

The other 1,397 are eating insects, fruit, nectar or pollen. The insect-eaters
are eating an amount of insects that would be genuinely difficult to overstate —
a single small bat can take thousands of insects in a night, which has obvious
implications for anyone who grows anything or dislikes being bitten.

## "They spread disease, so they're dangerous to have around"

This one needs care, because the honest answer isn't "no", it's "yes, in a
specific and manageable way".

Bats can carry diseases transmissible to humans, most notably rabies in some
regions. The practical risk to you personally, from bats you don't touch, is
extremely low. The rule is simple and it's the same rule you'd apply to any wild
mammal: **don't handle them.** If you find a grounded bat, call a local bat
group or wildlife rescue rather than picking it up.

Living near bats, watching bats, and listening to bats are not risky activities.
Handling them without training is, which is one of several good reasons why
handling requires a licence in many countries.

## "Bats are common, they're fine"

This is the one that does the most quiet damage, because it sounds reasonable.

Bats are among the more threatened groups of mammals. In North America,
white-nose syndrome has killed bats in numbers that are hard to comprehend —
[[White-Nose Syndrome|a fungal disease that wakes hibernating bats]] until they
burn through the fat reserves keeping them alive. Habitat loss, roost
destruction, wind turbines and light pollution all take a toll on top.

And here's the uncomfortable part: for a lot of species, in a lot of places,
**we don't actually know how they're doing**, because nobody has been counting.
"They seem fine" is often a statement about our data rather than about the bats.

## "There's nothing I can do about it"

There is, and it's unusually direct for a conservation problem.

- **Record what's near you.** Presence data from unsurveyed places is genuinely
  useful, and you don't need a qualification to collect it.
- **Leave roosts alone**, and tell someone who knows if you find one.
- **Turn lights off**, or at least point them down. Light pollution pushes bats
  off commuting routes.
- **Let your garden be slightly messier.** More insects, more bats. This is the
  laziest conservation action available and it genuinely works.
- **Tell someone.** The single most effective thing I've done for bats is hand a
  detector to a curious stranger for two minutes.

That last one is the whole reason this project exists, honestly. Nobody protects
what they've never experienced, and almost nobody has experienced a bat. Change
that and the rest gets easier.
