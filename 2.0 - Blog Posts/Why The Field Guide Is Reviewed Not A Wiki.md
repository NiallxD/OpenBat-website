The OpenBat field guide is written by the community. It is not, however,
a wiki. The difference is important, and in this post I will talk through why this is the case.

In short, anyone can propose changes or a new entry to the guide. But nobody can publish one on their own. Each change or addition goes through a review before it reaches anybody's phone.

## Why don't we just let people edit it?

When you maintain a dataset which can instantly be transmitted to hundreds or thousands of personal devices, it is critical that there is a human review step in the middle. It's not really about data accuracy, we trust our community know what they're talking about, it's about abuse. For this reason, manual review cannot, and will not, change.

There is something to be said for data accuracy, and that is a real risk, but that is precisely why a community sourced field guide is so powerful. Errors can be challenged by others, and through the process, corrected.

## What review actually looks like

What is the review process? It's nothing fancy. When a users submits a change or addition, a pull request is sent to me (Niall) via GitHub. This pull request tells me exactly what has been changed and I can choose if i want to 'merge' that change into the main file. If I do it goes live, if I don't it's rejected. I can also delegate this responsibility to other too. 

Your name goes on it, because the record of who contributed what is part of the
point. Use whatever name you're happy to see published.

## Which is why the guide starts small

There are around 1,500 bat species. We are not going to have a good entry for
each of them, and pretending otherwise would produce exactly the kind of thin,
confidently-wrong content this whole model exists to avoid.

So the guide is complete where we have the knowledge, and honest about where it
isn't. British Columbia is first, because that's where the project lives and where
the expertise we can actually reach is (I have the Bats of BC book on my desk right now!). Then it can expand outwards, at whatever pace real people can review and contribute.

That's an unglamorous roadmap. It's the difference between a field guide and a
pile of text, though, and I'd rather have the first one.

> [!tip] You know more than you think
> You don't need a degree to contribute. If you know that a species is common
> in your area, or that a photo is mislabelled, or that a measurement looks
> off, that's a useful submission. It's my job is to check nothing slipped through that shouldn't, not to be impressed.

If you want to have a go, the [Contribute](/contribute/) page is the way in, and if you have any questions please reach out, I don't bite!
