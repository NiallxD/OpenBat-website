OpenBat is a community project with an app attached. The app is free, and it
exists to do three things: build a field guide worth trusting, make bats easier
for ordinary people to appreciate, and turn a night of listening into data that
science can actually use.

## Three things that already exist, and nothing joining them

**Good ultrasonic microphones are now cheap.** Phil Atkin's open-source
[Griff Mini](https://www.pippyg.com/griff.html) design can be built for around
$20 CAD. The established detectors are fine instruments, but they put the whole
category behind several hundred dollars of proprietary hardware, and most
curious people stop there.

**Apps that let you listen already exist, and some of them record too.** What
none of them do well is the next step: turning that recording into an
observation someone else can use. So the moment passes, and either nothing is
kept or what is kept sits on a phone in a form nobody will accept.

**iNaturalist and NABat already exist and want the records.** Getting a bat
recording into them properly takes about ten minutes an observation and a
different device, so hardly anyone finishes.

OpenBat is the connective tissue between those three, not a competitor to any
one of them.

## What the app does

**It removes the handoff.** Every recording already carries its species, time,
place and measurements, and can be exported with the spectrogram alongside it.
The piece we are building now is the last step: one tap that turns a recording
into a complete observation — trimmed audio, a spectrogram drawn the way
reviewers expect, and standard GUANO metadata — ready to submit rather than a
file you will get around to sorting out.

**It lets you learn in the moment.** A cheap handheld detector lets you hear a
bat. It can't tell you anything about it. OpenBat pairs live on-device
identification with the field guide, narrowed to the species that actually live
where you are standing.

**It introduces people to community science who never set out to do any.** Most
people download it because they are curious about the noise over the garden.
Contributing is something the app suggests, not a club you have to join.

## Why simple records are worth having

Bird records sit on decades of dense data, so one more adds little. Bat
acoustic data starts close to empty across most of the world. "There are bats
here, on this date, at this time" is a genuine step forward in a place with
nothing.

That floor does not depend on the identification being right. Presence is the
contribution; a species name is a bonus when the confidence is there to support
it. iNaturalist's own research-grade standard works the same way — community
agreement at whatever level is achievable, not resolution to species.

## Free is a price. Open is a claim.

The microphone design, the guide data, the detection models, the app and the
output format are all open or published. Anyone can build, audit, extend or
replace any part of it without asking us. The app isn't tied to one vendor
either: any standard USB ultrasonic microphone that doesn't demand its own
companion app will work.

## Deliberately small

OpenBat is a few friends in Squamish, British Columbia. So the scope is honest
about that: the field guide is being completed for BC first while the app itself
stays free everywhere, it's iOS first with Android users pointed at other free
detectors in the meantime, and it plugs into the programmes that already exist —
the BC Community Bat Programs, NABat — rather than building another silo beside
them. Contributions to the guide are reviewed rather than published on sight.

The closest thing to a model for it isn't another bat project at all. It's
Merlin Bird ID feeding eBird: a free app that turns curiosity into records for a
much larger scientific platform, without being a conservation body itself.

The bat literature keeps naming the same two problems — not enough data, and
how people feel about bats. Both of them are addressed one person at a time, in
a garden, with a phone.
