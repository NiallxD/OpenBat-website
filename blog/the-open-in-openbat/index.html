The 'open' in OpenBat

OpenBat is free. But being 'free' is not the way we like to look at it, we like to say that OpenBat is Open. In this post we will talk a little bit about that philosophy and what it means.

1 September 2026 Niall Bell openbatproject

People are often surprised to see that OpenBat is free of charge. We charge nothing for the app, and we don’t include any in-app transactions. It is free. But being ‘free’ is not the way we like to look at it, we like to say that OpenBat is Open. In this post we will talk a little bit about that philosophy and what it means.

Free Does Not Mean Nothing

Things which are free have a price, the price is zero money. There a many apps which start off with a price of zero money only to later introduce a non-zero money price to use the app to it’s full potential. Some apps keep a price of zero money and instead you pay with something which isn’t money, your attention. The saying goes “if you don’t pay for the product, you are the product.” What this means is that the app isn’t the thing you buy, its the thing that allows the company to sell you. I’m talking about marketing, and data. Facebook didn’t make its billions by having a zero-money priced app did they?

OpenBat takes privacy serious. We avoid collecting information we don’t need and we choose not to operate servers which store data. If you’re worried about this claim, the code is available for you to read. And if you’re not a developer, you can paste the link to our source code into an ‘AI’ and it will do a decent job of auditing the code for privacy. Our source code is linked in the footer.

What About Open

Open is a different idea completely. Open-source is probably coming to mind here. Open-Source is used to describe software where the source code, that is the original code written, is made available to anyone, usually to use, tweak, and redeliver.

OpenBat is not quite open-source. We describe ourselves as ‘Open’ because we make our source code available to anyone who wants to read it and audit what we do. We share the methodology we used to arrive at decisions, and we implement open-source solutions in our software. We are also open to suggestions.

Why is this important. Well, nothing we are doing is strictly sellable. Anyone could recreate our code themselves using our methods totally legally. That isn’t a good business case. More importantly, we allow people to see what we are doing, and critique it. People can also learn from what we are doing, and help us learn too.

Our field guide is also open. Open to contribution. Our field guide is created by the community, and it is open for anyone to contribute.

Finally, your recordings are open to be moved around and played and analysed anywhere you like. The recordings are in WAV format and carry industry standard GUANO metadata.

This philosophy stops short of open-source for one good reason. We want to protect our software so that other people cannot profit from the ideas and approaches we have developed. Nothing here is strictly new, but we have brought together some interesting pieces, and we want to ensure that we can keep this free for everyone.

If you are interested in improving our code, working with us, or collaborating, let us know. We are open to all this! hello@openbat.app

The cost of being open

I’d be lying if I said it was all dandy.

Using ope-source models means that we inherit their limits. This could be the regions they being the only regions we can offer species ID in (currently North America, with the UK in beta) and we can’t magic up a model for new regions on demand, that’s not where our expertise is.

Community sourced data means the field guide can only grow at the rate at which people volunteering their knowledge. That’s slower than typing it in ourselves, even if we could, and much slower than letting a language model (AI) fill in 1,400 species profiles, which would be fast, cheap, and but also worthless.

Open, transparent, and privacy first.
